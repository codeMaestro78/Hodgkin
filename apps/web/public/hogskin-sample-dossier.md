## Short answer.

Evidence supports a modest, real reduction in sitting time when knowledge workers are given sit-stand desks — trial settings report roughly 30 to 60 fewer minutes of sitting per day [1]. Whether that buys sharper focus is less certain: measured effects on attention and self-reported productivity are small and inconsistent [2] [3]. The honest summary is that standing desks change posture more reliably than they change output.

## What holds up.

- Sit-stand arrangements reliably reduce occupational sitting across randomized trials, with the largest effects where organizations make posture change easy and normal rather than a stunt [1] [4].
- Benefits compound when desks are paired with regular movement breaks — standing still all day trades one sedentary posture for another [2].
- Musculoskeletal discomfort tends to drop when workers can alternate positions freely [3].

## What is overstated.

- Headlines promising large gains in concentration or weight loss outrun the trials. Measured cognition effects are near zero in the best-controlled studies [6].
- Prolonged static standing carries its own complaints — leg discomfort and, in susceptible people, vascular strain [3] [5].
- A desk is a posture option, not an exercise program [4].

## What we would test next.

- Trials that measure actual knowledge-work deliverables over months rather than lab attention tasks over days [6].
- Which work settings benefit most — deep-focus roles appear to prefer frequent posture changes, while collaborative roles may not [5].
